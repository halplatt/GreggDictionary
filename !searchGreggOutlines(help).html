Search Gregg Outlines Help

To find an outline, enter a word or part of a word (such as cat) and press Enter. To search stroke names, enter a pattern such as k-a-t and press Enter. The tool automatically chooses the search: entries containing a hyphen or slash search stroke names; other entries search text. You can also click Search Text or Search Strokes. That is all you need for a basic search.

Search Text

This is the most common type of search. By default, it looks for your text anywhere in a word (a Contains search). You can quickly change the search type to Exact match, Begins with, or Ends with.

Search Strokes

This searches the stroke names associated with outlines. It lets you search in reverse: if you know the strokes in an outline but do not know the word, search for those strokes to find words or phrases associated with the outline. For example, if you encounter an outline containing the strokes b-a, searching for them may reveal the phrase “been able,” even if you do not know what the outline means.

Most outlines are indexed by their stroke names, though not all. Many stroke names are intuitive; for details, open the ? Stroke Name Guide. A good way to learn the stroke names is to look up familiar words and compare their outlines with the names shown.

Like Search Text, Search Strokes defaults to Contains, finding the stroke sequence anywhere in an outline’s stroke-name sequence. You can change this to Exact match, Begins with, or Ends with.

The following apply to both Search Text and Search Strokes:

  • Search types
  • Results display options

  • Examples

Search Types

Choose how the search text or stroke sequence should match using the radio buttons:

  • Contains: match anywhere in the word or stroke-name sequence (the default).
  • Exact match: match the whole word or stroke-name sequence.
  • Begins with: match at the start of the word or stroke-name sequence.
  • Ends with: match at the end of the word or stroke-name sequence.

Changing the search type adds or removes the relevant start/end anchors in the search field and runs the search when the field is not empty. You can also type the anchors yourself: ^ means “start” and $ means “end.”

Results Display Options

You can control how many results are shown and how the outlines are displayed:

  • Maximum Words: controls how many matching results are displayed. It starts at 100.
  • Show Baseline: toggles the baseline guide drawn over outlines.
  • Show notes: displays notes when notes are available for the results.
  • Stroke Name Guide: opens the stroke-name reference.

Optional: Adding search patterns

You can search for ordinary text or stroke names without using patterns. To look for more specific combinations, optionally add special characters to your search. Think of them as extra clues that tell the search what to look for. You do not need to know about patterns to use the basic search.

The examples are optional. You can try the shortcut buttons, or skip this section and keep searching with ordinary words. Searches ignore letter case; by default, a pattern can match anywhere in a word or stroke text. The ^ and $ symbols mark the beginning and end.

PatternMeaningExample
.Any one character (except a line break).b.t matches “bat,” “bet,” or “bit.”
\wOne word character: a letter, digit, or underscore.b\wd matches “bad,” “bid,” or “bud.”
[aeiou]One character from the listed set.p[aeiou]t matches “pat,” “pet,” “pit,” and similar patterns.
[^aeiou]One character not in the listed set.p[^aeiou]t matches p, then a non-vowel, then t.
*Zero or more repetitions of the preceding character or group.^pea.*$ matches “pea” and words beginning with “pea.”
+One or more repetitions of the preceding character or group.^pea.+$ matches words beginning with “pea” plus at least one more character, such as “peak.”
?Zero or one occurrence of the preceding character or group.^colou?r$ matches “color” and “colour.”
|Either pattern on its left or right.^cat$|^dog$ matches “cat” or “dog.”
( )Groups characters so an operator can apply to the group.^(ha){2,}$ matches “haha,” “hahaha,” and so on.
{n}, {n,m}, {n,}Repeat the preceding character or group an exact number, within a range, or at least a number of times.^pea.{2}$ matches “peace.”

The buttons under Optional search pattern shortcuts insert symbols at the cursor position (or at the end of the text). They do not replace selected text. You can also type the symbols directly.

Search patterns use JavaScript's standard regular-expression syntax, a widely used industry standard. The search also supports advanced JavaScript regular-expression features if you want to use them; none are needed for ordinary searches.

Examples

Find words beginning with “pre”:

Choose Begins with and enter pre, or search with ^pre. This can find “prefix,” “prelude,” and “prevent.”

Find words ending in “ing”:

Choose Ends with and enter ing, or search with ing$. This can find “anything,” “awning,” and “bedspring.”

Find exactly “to”:

Choose Exact match and enter to, or use ^to$.

Find either of two complete words:

^single$|^double$ matches “single” or “double.”

You can start with ordinary words and add pattern symbols as needed. For complex patterns, test a small search first and adjust the pattern based on the results.
